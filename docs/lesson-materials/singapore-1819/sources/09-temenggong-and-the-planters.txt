SOURCE 9: The Temenggong's settlement and the gambier planters

Type: (c) Prepared summary of one historian's argument, with short quotations of the early British records he cites. Includes (a) a short extract from Raffles's report of February 1819 as quoted in that article.
Prepared: 11 November 2026.
Based on: Jason Heng, "An Old Teochew Oral Account Sheds New Light on the 1819 Founding of Singapore", BiblioAsia (Chapters on Asia, published 30 June 2018); C. B. Buckley, An Anecdotal History of Old Times in Singapore (1902), volume 1, entry for 1824 (Crawfurd's report of January 1824).
Reference URLs: https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/chapters-of-asia/chapters-on-asia-2014-2017-teochew-1819-founding-singapore/ ; https://archive.org/details/ananecdotalhist00buckgoog

The Temenggong. Temenggong Abdul Rahman "moved to Singapore with 150 followers in 1811 and lived by the north bank of the Singapore River ... until the British compelled the relocation of his istana (palace) to Telok Blangah in 1823." When Raffles asked to land soldiers and raise the flag, the Temenggong said, according to the diary of Captain John Crawford, that "nothing could give them greater happiness than to be in alliance with the English". Heng argues that "Temenggong Abdul Rahman's agenda was not military, but trade through the creation of a rival port in Singapore", pointing to his share of duties in the 1819 treaty (Source 5).

Plantations before the British. Farquhar's letters show "various Malays and Chinese" whom the Temenggong "had granted leave to clear ground for plantations, of which about 20 had commenced prior to British establishment." The June 1819 arrangement left "[T]he gardens and plantations that now are, or may hereafter be made" "at the disposal of the Tumungong, as heretofore". Gambier, used in betel chewing, "was significant as the primary export product of nearby Riau before the mid-19th century." Crawford's diary records "upwards of 100" Chinese offering to work as labourers near the British tents.

Raffles's own report, 13 February 1819 (as quoted by Heng): "the industrious Chinese are already established in the interior and may soon be expected to supply vegetables, and c., and c., equal to the demand." He adds that "a large part of the population are already engaged in building boats and vessels". Heng's comment: "Without question these were commercial activities beyond the scale of an "insignificant fishing village"".

By 1823-1824. "By November 1821, Singapore was reported to have had a "regularly built Chinese town" on the south bank of the Singapore River". In January 1824 Crawfurd reported that the Chinese "were of two classes, Macao and Hokien", and that "all the merchants and most of the good agriculturists were Hokien" (Buckley).

Heng's conclusion: "Raffles managed to whitewash the roles of Temenggong Abdul Rahman and his Teochew partners" behind Singapore's rise.

Source limitation: This is one historian's argument, built partly on a Teochew oral tradition that Heng admits "is not without deficiency". The British records he cites (Farquhar's letters, Raffles's report, Crawford's diary) were checked only as he quotes them. Other accounts in this pack disagree: Wa Hakim remembered "no houses in the island except at Kampong Tĕmĕnggong" (Source 3). The exact number of planters in 1819 is not known. Crawfurd's view of which Chinese were "the most respectable" is his own opinion. The game should present the planters as evidence that people were already working and trading here, and Heng's wider claims as an interpretation.
