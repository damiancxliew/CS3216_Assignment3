From the Malay Annals (Sejarah Melayu), Leyden's translation, 1821:
"thus Sang Nila Utama settled the country of Tamasak, named it Singhapura, and reigned over it" (page 44)
"The country of Singhapura is of great extent, and frequented by merchants innumerable from every quarter, and its ports are very populous." (page 45)
Of its first king: he "was buried on the hill of Singhapura" (page 48)

What historians say:
- The old name. The island appears as "Tumasik in an old Javanese poem in 1365" and as Danmaxi in the account of the Chinese trader Wang Dayuan (Roots). The Malay Annals call it Singapura.
- A 14th-century port. Tan Tai Yong writes: "In the 14th century, Singapore was already a centre for a vast trading network and actively engaged in commerce with neighbouring ports and regions." Hornbill casques and lakawood "were exported from Singapore, or Temasek, as it was known then." Wang Dayuan "notes that Chinese people lived alongside orang laut natives at Longyamen".
- The hill. In January 1984 the National Museum invited the archaeologist John Miksic to dig on Fort Canning Hill; "The team had found evidence that a 14th century settlement really had existed on Fort Canning Hill" (Roots). Derek Heng writes that the finds "point to the existence of a late-13th to 14th-century settlement on the hill". Javanese-style gold ornaments were also found on the hill.
- The stone at the river mouth. The Singapore Stone stood at the mouth of the river; it "has inscriptions in the Kawi script and some Sanskrit words, and has been dated to between the 10th and 14th centuries", and it "was eventually blown up in 1843 for the British fort and quarters" (Roots).
- Decline. "By the 15th century, Singapore had declined as an international trading port in the face of the ascendance of the Malacca Sultanate" (Heng, 2005). "In 1613, the Portuguese raided and burned the island to the ground" (Roots, "A Game of Thrones").
- Who lived at the river. "The Orang Laut, "sea people" in Malay, were the earliest known inhabitants around the river, living in sampans at its mouth, and in a village called Kampong Temenggong, located at the north bank of the river, along with other Malays." (Roots)
- Raffles knew the story. A copy of the Malay Annals was "likely transcribed for Stamford Raffles in 1816" (Heng, 2020), and in his letter of June 1819 Raffles mentions "the ruins of the ancient Capital of "Singapura"" (Source 1).
- 200 or 700 years? In 2019, the 200th anniversary year, BiblioAsia's editor wrote that "Singapore's history did not begin with Raffles' arrival in 1819: it goes back some 500 years earlier."

Source limitation: The Malay Annals were written down centuries after the events (Heng names a version dated 1612) and mix history with legend: the lion and the founding prince cannot be checked, and Heng writes that "the historicity of the pre-Melakan era, in which the Singapura period falls, cannot be verified by other sources" except one Chinese text. Leyden's translation is old and loose. The archaeology shows a 14th-century settlement and trade, not the details of the legends. The hosts disagree on when the gold ornaments were found: BiblioAsia says "recovered in 1926 at Fort Canning", Roots says they were "discovered by a group of Chinese workers at Fort Canning Hill in 1928". The Leyden wording was checked against two scans on archive.org; curly quotation marks are written as straight ones.
