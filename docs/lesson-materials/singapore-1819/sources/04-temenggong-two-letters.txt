SOURCE 4: The Temenggong's two letters, and what he said about the island

Type: (a) Verbatim primary extract, in an English translation made by the British Resident, William Farquhar, as printed in 1902; with (c) a later historian's account of the first meeting.
Prepared: 11 November 2026.
Based on: Charles Burton Buckley, An Anecdotal History of Old Times in Singapore (Singapore, 1902), volume 1, pages 29-31 and 51-52.
Reference URL: https://archive.org/details/ananecdotalhist00buckgoog (page images: https://archive.org/download/ananecdotalhist00buckgoog/page/30_w800.jpg and 52_w800.jpg)

1. The declaration of 1 March 1819 (Buckley, page 52), signed by Sultan Hussein and Temenggong Abdul Rahman, translated by Farquhar. Speaking in the first person, the Temenggong says:
"I hereby freely acknowledge that I did write a letter to Mr. Adrian Koek of Malacca, and one to the Rajah Mudah of Rhio, to the above effect, but my motive for so writing arose solely from the apprehension of bringing on me the vengeance of the Dutch at some future period. But I here call God and His Prophet to witness that the English established themselves at Singapore with my free will and consent: and that from the arrival of the Honorable Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles no troops or effects were landed, or anything executed but with the free accord of myself and of the Sultan of Johore."
"The above effect" refers to letters "intimating that the factory which the English have recently established here was forcibly formed entirely against my will".
Farquhar's covering letter says the Dutch Governor of Malacca had reported that the British settlement had been made "in a forcible manner without the previous consent of the Local Authorities of the country".

2. The first meeting, late January 1819, as told by Buckley in 1902 (page 30). Buckley combines Wa Hakim's and Munshi Abdullah's accounts with his own guesses. He writes that the Temenggong "said the question of the succession to the late Sultan was still unsettled, and that Tunku Putri had all the regalia; but he was the inheritor of the island by the Malayan law." Asked the name of the hill behind the plain (today's Fort Canning), "he said it was called Bukit Larangan, "Because the Rajah resided there in old times and erected his palace there, and would allow no one to go up, so it was called the Forbidden Hill.""

3. On pages 29-30, Buckley reports that "the Tumongong four years afterwards told Mr. Crawfurd that 150 Malays had accompanied him from Johore in 1811."

Source limitation: The declaration survives only in Farquhar's English translation, made for the British to answer the Dutch complaint, so the British had every reason to want it to say this. It shows the Temenggong saying two different things to two different audiences: to the Dutch, that the British forced him; to the British, that he agreed freely. Both letters show his fear of Dutch revenge. Which was true, or whether both were partly true, the sources do not settle. Jason Heng (Source 9) suggests the Temenggong had his own reasons to welcome a rival port to Dutch-held Riau. Buckley's account of the first meeting was written more than 80 years later and he says himself that parts are guesswork (page 31: "We cannot doubt that Raffles spent the afternoon in talking to the Tumongong"); he does not say where the Temenggong's words about the hill come from. Buckley also repeats insulting colonial descriptions of the Orang Laut, which should not be copied into the game.
