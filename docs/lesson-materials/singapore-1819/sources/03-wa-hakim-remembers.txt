SOURCE 3: An Orang Laut eyewitness remembers the landing

Type: (b) Later recollection by a participant. The statement of Wa Hakim, an Orang Laut man who was about fifteen in 1819, recorded about sixty years later and printed in 1882. Extracts; "..." marks words left out.
Prepared: 11 November 2026.
Based on: "Landing of Raffles in Singapore. By an Eye-Witness", Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, No. 10 (1882), pages 285-286, signed "H. T. H." (BiblioAsia names the recorder as H. T. Haughton and dates the issue June 1882). Quoted in Tan Tai Yong, "Looking Back at 700 Years of Singapore", BiblioAsia 14:4 (2019).
Reference URL: https://archive.org/details/in.gov.ignca.25036 (page images: https://archive.org/download/in.gov.ignca.25036/page/285_w800.jpg and 286) ; second scan: https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.70586 ; https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-14/issue-4/jan-mar-2019/looking-back-at-sg/

The editor's introduction (page 285): the account is printed "as going to prove how unreliable is the detailed account, given in the "Hikâiat Abdullah," from what Abdullah was told a few months afterwards."

"Wa Hakim, now residing in Tĕluk Saga, of the Kĕlûmang tribe of "Orang laut," was, according to his own account, about fifteen years old when Sir Stamford Raffles landed, so he must be about eighty years old at the present time."

His statement (pages 285-286):
"At the time when Tuan Raffles came, there were under one hundred small houses and huts at the mouth of the river [Singapore]; but the Râja's house was the only large one, and it stood back from the river, between the sea and the river, near the obelisk. About thirty families of 'Orang laut' also lived in boats ... a little way up the Singapore river at the wide part ... About half the 'Orang laut' lived ashore and half in boats. My sister still lives in a boat there, and has never lived ashore."
"I myself was born in the Singapore waters, and this settlement of Malays and 'Orang laut' was in existence in my earliest recollection. [Crawfurd says it was first made in 1811, and Wa Hakim's recollection confirms this statement.]"
"The men that lived in boats were the first to see Tuan Raffles coming. I remember the boat landing in the morning."
"They were entertained by the Tĕmĕnggong and he gave them rambutans and all kinds of fruit."
"Batin Sapi, an Orang laut, went to bring Tunku Long from Bulang."
"I myself helped to cut them down and assisted in making the fort (kubu) and digging a trench between Tuan Raffles' house and the sea."
"There were no houses in the island except at Kampong Tĕmĕnggong."

Source limitation: Wa Hakim spoke about sixty years after the events, in Malay, and an English writer translated and arranged his words, adding notes in square brackets. Some details are doubtful: he says the tents were pitched "about twelve days afterwards", which C. B. Buckley (1902) calls "out of the question" (the tents came ashore on 29 January). His "under one hundred small houses and huts" disagrees with Munshi Abdullah's "four or five small huts" (Source 12), and his "no houses in the island except at Kampong Temenggong" disagrees with Farquhar's report of plantations already started (Source 9). He was a boy following the visitors "to the edge of the verandah", so he did not hear what was agreed inside. The wording was checked by eye against the scanned page images; curly quotation marks are written as straight ones, the printed accents (Tĕmĕnggong, Râja, Hikâiat, Kĕlûmang) are kept except the one on "Batin", which is hard to read in the scan, and two Malay phrases in brackets are left out.
