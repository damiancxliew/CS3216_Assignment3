1. The rule. Regulation No. II of 1823, "A Regulation for the Port of Singapore" (Memoir, appendix page 41):
"The port of Singapore is a free port, and the trade thereof is open to ships and vessels of every nation free of duty, equally and alike to all."

2. Raffles on duties, letter to Lord Lansdowne, 19 January 1821 (as quoted in BiblioAsia, 2016):
"In point of Commercial importance it already rivals Batavia, and its whole charge scarcely exceeds £10,000 a year, ten times which amount might be collected were I to allow of the Collection of even moderate duties."

3. Why here. The Malacca Strait lay "at an important crossroad along the East-West trade route between the South China Sea and the Indian Ocean" (Tan Tai Yong). "Singapore lies at the confluence of seasonal winds and monsoons which ships relied on for travel" (Roots). Its port drew on three networks: Chinese, Southeast Asian, and "the European and Indian Ocean network" (Heng). In 1818 Raffles complained that "The Dutch possess the only passes through which ships must sail into the Archipelago, the straits of Sunda and Malacca" (quoted by Tan Tai Yong).

4. Why traders came. Raffles told Farquhar "not to impose tariffs" in 1819, and "Traders flocked to Singapore, drawn by the promises of no tariffs and low port fees." Not everything was free: "The Malay rajas in Singapore demanded gifts from the nakhoda [ship captains] but by April 1820, with Raffles' reforms, the nakhoda had won the right not to offer gifts to them." (Abel) Buckley records small port charges by vessel size from May 1820, and a proclamation of November 1822 that trade was free to all, "published by beat of gong" in local languages. In 1820 almost 500 Bugis moved from Dutch Riau to Singapore, and "Their arrival shifted the lucrative Bugis trade westwards, away from Dutch Riau and towards British Singapore." (Khoo)

5. The numbers (as reported by Buckley). Raffles reported that in the first two and a half years "2889 vessels had entered and cleared, of which 383 were owned and commanded by Europeans, and 2,506 by natives, and that their united tonnage was 161,000 tons." For 1822: "The total tonnage, importing and exporting in 1822, was 130,689 tons. The total value of imports and exports was $8,568,172." Buckley also reports that duties and harbour fees collected at Dutch Malacca fell from $50,000 in 1819 to $23,000 in 1821, and to $7,000 two years later.

Source limitation: Raffles's statements are those of the founder of the free port, writing to win support; he gives free trade the credit and says nothing about the revenue farms that paid many of the costs (Source 10). The trade figures come from Raffles's reports as retold by Buckley in 1902, and were not checked against the original records; they count native craft and European ships together, so "2,889 vessels" includes many small boats (Buckley notes the average was 56 tons). The fall in Malacca's duties shows trade moving, but other causes may have played a part. The Lansdowne letter was checked as quoted by BiblioAsia, not against the original. The Memoir wording was checked against two scans of the 1830 book; Buckley's figures were read from the archive.org transcription of the 1902 book, where some symbols are misread, so the Malacca figures and port charges are given here in summary rather than quotation. Curly quotation marks are written as straight ones.
