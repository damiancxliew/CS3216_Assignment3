SOURCE 10: Paying for the settlement: Farquhar, Raffles and the revenue farms, 1819-1823

Type: (c) Prepared summary of two secondary accounts that take different sides, with short quotations.
Prepared: 11 November 2026.
Based on: Nadia Wright, "Farquhar & Raffles: The Untold Story", BiblioAsia 14:4 (January-March 2019); C. B. Buckley, An Anecdotal History of Old Times in Singapore (1902), volume 1, pages 60-64, 72-73 and 105-106; Tan Tai Yong, "Looking Back at 700 Years of Singapore", BiblioAsia 14:4 (2019).
Reference URLs: https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-14/issue-4/jan-mar-2019/fnr-untold-story/ ; https://archive.org/details/ananecdotalhist00buckgoog ; https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-14/issue-4/jan-mar-2019/looking-back-at-sg/

Who ran Singapore. "Farquhar administered Singapore for nearly four-and-a-half years between 7 February 1819 and 1 May 1823, while Raffles was present for barely eight months during those years: from 31 May to 27 June 1819 and returning on 10 October 1822." (Wright)

Little money. In October 1819, worried that the settlement might be handed back to the Dutch, Lord Hastings "imposed severe reductions on costs and personnel" (Wright).

Revenue farms. A "farm" was the right to run a trade, sold to the highest bidder. Wright: "As the population increased, so did the crime rate – largely due to gambling and opium smoking. Farquhar planned to rein in these activities by selling licences for the sale of arrack (a local alcoholic spirit) and opium, and for the running of gaming houses. This would also generate revenue which he could use to pay for a much-needed police force." Buckley gives the first monthly figures, from 1820: $395 "for four opium shops, $160 for arrack shops, and $95 for gaming tables", spent on police and on payments to the Sultan and Temenggong for helping with police duties.

What Raffles thought: the two accounts disagree.
- Buckley: when Farquhar proposed opium and spirit farms, "Raffles wrote from Bencoolen to say that he considered it highly objectionable" and "inapplicable to the principles on which the establishment at Singapore was founded".
- Wright: Raffles himself issued instructions for opium licences, and "he also took a 5 percent commission on each opium licence for himself". She adds that "the opium farms "introduced" by Farquhar and sanctioned by Raffles became Singapore's largest single source of revenue from 1824 until 1910".

Free trade. Farquhar "highlighted Singapore's free trade status – although Raffles intended this to be only a temporary measure" (Wright). See Source 11.

The quarrel. Farquhar let merchants build warehouses on the north bank of the river, land Raffles had kept for government; Farquhar said that otherwise Singapore would have "completely withered in the bud" (Wright). In 1822 Raffles "relied on the inexperienced Philip Jackson for engineering advice" (Wright) and "instructed assistant engineer Philip Jackson to draw up a plan for the town of Singapore", "with different neighbourhoods laid out for the Chinese, Malays, Bugis and Indians, as well as a dedicated European Town by the Singapore River" (Tan Tai Yong). "Raffles saw Singapore as a British port, while Farquhar regarded it as a Malay port that belonged to the Malay rulers." "Farquhar expressed concern when Raffles began to sell land, pointing out that Raffles had no authority to do so as the land rightfully belonged to the Malays." (Wright) Buckley records that when Bugis traders sold slaves in the river in 1822, "The Resident replied that he allowed the practice", which Raffles later gave as a reason for distrusting him.

The end. "On 1 May 1823, Raffles dismissed Farquhar as Resident and took over control of Singapore." Wright adds: "He had no authority to do so as Hastings was the one who had appointed Farquhar." Raffles abolished slavery by a regulation of 1 May 1823, and "The gaming licenses were stopped in May" 1823 (Buckley). When Farquhar left at the end of 1823, "He received heartfelt farewell addresses from the Bugis, Chinese and Indian communities" (Wright).

Source limitation: The two main accounts take sides. Wright's article was written, in the words of its introduction, because "Nadia Wright claims that his role has been exaggerated at the expense of another": it defends Farquhar. Buckley (1902) mostly follows Raffles's papers. On whether Raffles opposed or allowed the opium farms they disagree directly, and both may be partly right at different times. The money figures are Buckley's and were not checked against the original records. The game must not invent any further figures for revenue or costs.
