SOURCE 6: "Rightfully theirs": the Dutch view of Singapore, 1818-1824

Type: (c) Prepared summary of secondary accounts. No Dutch document is quoted directly: the short quotations below are the words of modern historians, or of British writers reporting Dutch complaints.
Prepared: 11 November 2026.

Before Raffles came. On 28 November 1818, by a treaty with Raja Jaafar, the Dutch were permitted to re-occupy Riau (Heng). Singapore was part of the Johor Empire, "and its ruler, Sultan Abdul Rahman, had sworn allegiance to the Dutch" (Wright). Raffles learned in Penang "that the Dutch were claiming all territories under the Johor Sultanate" (Roots). Heng notes "Dutch representations of pre-1819 Singapore as a "den of murderers" and of Temenggong Abdul Rahman as the "head of the pirates"". Heng also writes that "until 1819 Riau fulfilled the role of the much-needed entrepôt on the Singapore Straits".

The Dutch case against Raffles. The Dutch "argued that Singapore, as part of Riau's territories, was rightfully theirs" (BiblioAsia, 2016). They challenged the legality of the settlement, "stating that the island had always been within their sphere of influence" (Roots). The Dutch Governor-General, Baron van der Capellen, "continued to insist that Sultan Hussein had no right to allow the British to establish a post, and demanded that the British withdraw from Singapore" (Wright). Farquhar reported in March 1819 that the Dutch Governor of Malacca had written that the British post was made "in a forcible manner without the previous consent of the Local Authorities of the country" (Buckley).

The danger. "The Dutch protested, and reports were received that they would retake Singapore by force" (Wright). The Dutch letters "made the British authorities initially disavow Raffles's schemes" (Khoo).

How it ended. In 1820, after Dutch soldiers in Riau mistook wedding gunfire for a revolt, many Bugis left Riau for Singapore; Farquhar wrote that the Bugis "have lost all confidence in their [the Dutch] system of government" (Khoo). By the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 17 March 1824, the Dutch withdrew their objections to the British occupation of Singapore (Source 5).

Source limitation: This is the Dutch view as described by Singapore and British writers; no Dutch-language document was checked, and the Dutch protests themselves were not found in a form that could be quoted. The phrases "den of murderers" and "head of the pirates" are Dutch descriptions as reported by Heng, and they are hostile to the Temenggong. Nothing found shows the Dutch calling the island empty or worthless, so the game must not put those words in a Dutch mouth. The sources agree that the Dutch claimed Singapore through their recognition of Sultan Abdul Rahman and their control of Riau.
