SOURCE V2-07: The other side of the strait: a bluff, or a calculated risk?

Type: (b) Later recollections by two Japanese commanders, quoted at second or third hand, with (c) a prepared summary of historians' arguments.

Yamashita's later words, as quoted by Headrick from a 1966 book:

"My attack on Singapore was a bluff"

"I was very frightened all the time that the British would discover our numerical weakness"

Headrick writes that Yamashita had about 30,000 men left against roughly 100,000 defenders, with ammunition running low. Finest Hour describes Yamashita's force on 5 February as "down to eighteen tanks and lacking ammunition and food".

Colonel Tsuji, a Japanese staff officer, wrote in his memoir that soldiers riding bicycles on bare rims at night "made a noise resembling that of tanks".

The historian Brian Farrell sees it differently. The Japanese "driving charge" forced the army "to run very serious risks with its supply lines", kept going by captured stores that Tsuji called "Churchill supplies". Farrell reports the Japanese historian Akashi Yoji's view that this was "a premeditated calculated risk". Of "Yamashita’s loosely worded claim he bluffed Percival", Farrell writes: "I dismissed this for the nonsense it was."

Source limitation: No Japanese wartime record was inspected. Yamashita's words come through a 1966 book quoted in a 1994 student paper; Farrell calls Tsuji's memoir "self-serving". Figures differ: Percival estimated about 60,000 Japanese (Source V2-05). These accounts were written after the victory, by men with reputations to make or defend.
