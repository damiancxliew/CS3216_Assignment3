# PRD v3: Rationale

The reasoning behind [`PRD-v3.md`](PRD-v3.md). The previous PRD and the early gameplay notes (both in
[`archive/`](archive/)) and the 6 Oct feedback (teacher meeting and `NUS_App_Feedback.docx`) may be consulted as
reference. Nothing from them enters the v3
documents without Kevin's approval.

## Method

Design from the highest level down, and agree each level before going below it. We expect a rewrite of large
parts of the code. The existing code is useful as infrastructure that is already set up.

## Decisions (approved)

V3 and V5 were merged into V13 and V14 and are retired. Where a later decision refines an earlier one, the earlier
row says so.

| # | Decision | Rationale | Requirements |
|---|---|---|---|
| V1 | The teacher's pack sets the focus; the wider historical record supplies the substance. *How* it is sourced is decided at level 7. | A source pack is almost always far too thin to build a convincing situation from. | G1, G1.1 |
| V2 | The clock is a teacher setting, not a design pillar. | Time pressure is not the pressure we want. | P1 |
| V4 | Holistic aims, not historian training. | The aim is critical, reflective, curious thinkers, not professional historians. Differing interpretations are presented as "people read this differently, and here's why". | E3.2 |
| V6 | The game ends with an in-character judgement (now the Interview, V40), then the reveal. There is no extra prompt after the reveal. Teachers can add their own questions. | The judgement speaks from inside the student's own choices. It goes at the end of the game, not each stage, because it needs evidence from every stage and shouldn't break the flow. | E2, E3, T1 |
| V7 | Stages are sequential, and choices carry forward. | This makes causes and consequences across stages teachable. Stages don't need large time gaps between them (see V30). | G2, G2.1 |
| V8 | Web and external sources ship before the pilot. | Follows from V1. | G1.1 |
| V9 | The world pushes back. | Testing reasoning means getting feedback, not a score. | P2, P2.1 |
| V10 | Predict, then reveal. | The gap between prediction and outcome teaches without grading. | P3, P3.1 |
| V11 | Thinking is played back to the student and summarised for the teacher. | This is part of the approved purpose statement. | E3.3, T2 |
| V12 | By default, a full session (stages, epilogue, Interview and reveal) fits into 45 minutes. | Lessons don't always start on time, and the teacher may want to speak, so a 45–60 minute lesson needs a buffer. This is a default only (see V32). | C1, P1.4 |
| V13 | "Pressure" means: no option satisfies everyone, and not everything can be known. | These are the pressures historical actors really faced, and they make students think rather than hurry. | — |
| V14 | No exam-style questions during play; any exam element comes at the end of the game. | Keeps play from feeling like a test. | C2 |
| V15 | Support Sec through JC reading levels. | Both are target audiences. | C3 |
| V16 | Never grade or rank students. | Part of the purpose statement. | C4 |
| V17 | The teacher decides whether the timer is on. The student can always end a stage by deciding. When a timer runs out, a final call happens in the story: the decision is forced, and the student still chooses. | The timer is a teaching choice, not a cost control. A forced final call paces without punishing, unlike an automatic pass. | P1, P1.1, P1.2 |
| V18 | Cost is capped by an abuse rate limit above human speed and a generous per-attempt ceiling, not by throttling characters. | A character reply costs about S$0.00026 (measured 25 Sep, simulated attempts). Typing speed caps a real student at cents per hour, even at 5–10× today's per-turn cost. The real risk is scripts, and forgotten tabs cost nothing. Deflections, action caps and low-key prompts hurt the experience more than they save. | C5, C6, C7 |
| V19 | Labelling: provenance that fits the world during play, and an honest breakdown at the end of the game. During play: period-appropriate document details, a subtle mark on real material, the source on request, and no citations in dialogue. At the end: the teacher's materials, other sources, invented content, and the student's own path. The teacher sees every label before publishing. Facts without a citation count as invented. | The record has to stay separate from the simulation, but citing everywhere breaks immersion and feels like an AI chat. Provenance that fits the period is also what source skills need (who wrote it, when, why). The model's own knowledge can't be checked, so it isn't treated as documented. | G1.2, G1.2.1, P4, P4.1, P4.2, E3.4, T3 |
| V20 | The teacher sets the timer when creating an adventure. It defaults to on, with stage lengths adding up to 45 minutes of play. | Superseded in part by V12: the default now fits the whole session, including the epilogue, Interview and reveal, into 45 minutes. | P1.3, P1.4 |
| V21 | Understanding the episode is the primary learning outcome. Critical thinking is what the game develops along the way. | The teacher's own measure of success is that students grasp the concerns, conflicts and risks, and why things turned out as they did. The feedback's "insights tracker" measures exactly that. Skills without understanding leave students sharp but unsure what happened. | Learning outcomes |
| V22 | Critical thinking is broken into five parts: question claims (including close reading), cross-check, consider other perspectives (then and since), reason about consequences, and weigh and reach a judgement. | Checked against the feedback: slanting and withholding characters, conflicting sources with provenance, persona depth, the causal map, competing interpretations, the evaluative question, and the teacher's "top three priorities" mechanic. | Learning outcomes |
| V23 | Connecting the past to the present is not a game outcome. | The teacher covers it in class. | — |
| V24 | Understanding the episode wins any trade-off with the skills. | The outcomes support each other, but they pull apart in specific choices: slanting characters, biased documents, time spent going deep versus covering everything, and how much hints give away. | Learning outcomes |
| V25 | Characters and documents may mislead during play, but whatever misled the student is corrected by the end of the game. | Misleading content is what exercises critical thinking. Understanding comes first, so no student leaves with a false picture. | E3.5 |
| V26 | The skills follow the rhythm of a stage (during, end, next stage, end of game). There is no ranking among the skills. | Every skill is practised every stage. The stage decision is itself a judgement, so ranking judgement low didn't make sense. | Learning outcomes |
| V27 | Reflection (noticing how your own view changed) is not a learning outcome. | It isn't easily measurable and isn't worth designing for. | — |
| V28 | Seven pillars (see the PRD). They settle design trade-offs at every lower level. | Each comes from an approved decision. 1 from V24/V25; 2 from the hint and secret-ending discussion, with "never graded" (V16) folded in; 3 because fleshed-out characters serve both outcomes; 4 from V9; 5 from V13/V2/V17; 6 from V19. 7 because the current game makes every step trivial (walk up, ask anything, the goal completes), while exploring stays valuable and walking is thinking time. | Pillars |
| V29 | The player role must have an identity, stakes, a private brief, real access and decisions that matter, whatever the role is. | The current role (effectively a random office worker) feels pointless because it has none of these, not because it is fictional. Real and fictional roles can both meet them. | G3–G3.4 |
| V30 | The episode sets the number of stages, and it can be one. Stages don't need large time gaps between them. | Some episodes are a single moment. Requiring big jumps in time would be a heavy constraint on the generator and on teachers' choice of episode. For one-stage adventures, consequences and predictions are resolved in the reveal. | G2 |
| V31 | Students are guided by gradually more specific hints during a stage, plus the timer if the teacher turned it on. | Some guidance and pacing is needed without rushing or doing the work for the student. Hints are designed in V35, V52 and V61. | P5 |
| V32 | Time is the teacher's decision. The design is not otherwise built around time limits; only default timings are set. | Take-home use has no lesson to fit. Keep it simple. | P1, P1.3, P1.4, C1 |
| V33 | The teacher has broad power to reshape the generated adventure, including the number of stages. Details are designed in V71. | The generator's choices won't always suit the lesson. | T4 |
| V34 | Relationships: each character has 3–5 stances specific to them, changed only by triggers written at generation. The model expresses each stance freely. A hidden top stance can be reached through good play. | Anything the game must judge reliably is written in advance and detected during play. A score updated every message would be noisy and easy to game (flattery). A small set of states is the industry norm (WoW reputation, Fire Emblem supports, Persona social links). The depth comes from free expression, meaningful reasons for a change, what a change unlocks, and different characters reacting differently. Character-specific stances read as people, not meters. | G4, G4.1, P6, P6.1, P6.2 |
| V35 | Progress engine: discoveries written in advance with reveal conditions, confirmed during play by a cheap checker. The student sees only the stage's central question and their notes. Open questions stay hidden and surface only as hints when the student is stuck. | Highly feasible: judgements are made against a short written list. The character decides whether to reveal, and the checker only confirms it happened. Hiding the questions makes students form their own (questioning claims, curiosity) and avoids a checklist. The central question and quick hints answer the NUS complaint that students were "not sure how to succeed". | G5, G5.1, G5.2, P5.1–P5.3, P7–P7.3 |
| V36 | The student's notes are the source of truth for what they know. Discoveries are earned only when a character conveys them. Information is passed on only by inserting a note into the chat. Asserting something undiscovered makes the character question its basis, and changes nothing in the game. | Stops students skipping the game by typing what they read elsewhere or heard from a classmate, while prior knowledge isn't punished. Challenging the basis is fairer than silently ignoring it, and is itself critical thinking ("how do you know?"). A tool inside the chat keeps passing on information deterministic without leaving the conversation. | P8–P8.3 |
| V37 | Each stage combines continuous influence (discoveries, stance changes, information passed on) with a defining moment: a scene in the story where a real figure decides based on what the student advises, conveys or keeps back. The student chooses from 2–4 written go-between acts. The historical outcome is offered at each defining moment while reachable, and never labelled (narrowed by V54). | A go-between doesn't make historical decisions ("who are you to decide?"), but controls information, framing and advice. Real figures keep the real decisions, so history stays clean. The defining moment keeps the "weigh and judge" phase, the felt pressure, a moment for the prediction, and a point the class can compare. Written acts are more reliable than interpreting free text. Offering the historical act while it is reachable keeps the comparison with history close at hand (V54). | P1.1, P9–P9.2 |
| V38 | Consequences are deterministic: written rules pick one of 3–4 outcomes for each defining moment, minor details vary, and contingency is taught in the reveal and through secret endings. | The most explainable, testable and teacher-reviewable option, and it avoids "probabilities that read as historical fact". Deterministic outcomes risk suggesting history was inevitable, so the reveal discusses how things could have gone differently, and secret endings show other paths. Varying details gives variety without unfairness. Rules stay small (a few acts × a few conditions → 3–4 outcomes). Dice felt unfair and were hard to explain; a model deciding was less predictable and harder to test. | G6, G6.1, P9.3, P9.4, E3.6 |
| V39 | The student starts the defining moment when ready (the timer can bring it to them). Core discoveries are marked: hints steer towards them, a character nudges without blocking if they're missing, and the reveal covers them. Characters react immediately to the student's act, then come the prediction and the consequences. | Freedom without leaving anyone out (understanding first, low floor). Nudging rather than blocking avoids a return to the checklist. The immediate reaction makes the world push back at the most important moment. | G5.3, P5.4, P9.5–P9.7, E3.7 |
| V40 | The end of the game is Epilogue → Interview → Reveal. The epilogue shows how the student's run ended, labelled as their simulation. The Interview is a short in-chat conversation, essentially a gamified reflection: a character fitting the ending asks a question; the student picks a verdict, backs it by inserting notes, optionally adds words; the interviewer pushes back once; the student can respond or change their verdict. | A written "testimony" would be the least fun part and feel like homework, and the word is hard to decode. A conversation uses verbs students already know. Defending a verdict against push-back is the judgement skill and "the world pushes back" in one moment. Choosing plus attaching notes is the low floor; arguing in their own words is the high ceiling. Each ending needs its own question, because "could separation have been prevented?" makes no sense if separation never happened in that run. | E1, E2–E2.5, G6.2 |
| V41 | Pausing and resuming is low priority. | In class there's time, and take-home has no limit, so it's rarely needed. | P10 |
| V42 | Default time split: about 2 minutes of briefing, 30 minutes of stages, 1 of epilogue, 4 of Interview, 6 of reveal (about 43 in total). The generator prefers 1–2 stages. The reveal shows a short core by default, with more on request. | About 10 minutes per stage allows only about 15–25 exchanges once walking, reading and model replies are counted, so 3 stages would be shallow. Seven reveal items won't fit in 6 minutes; the teacher can cover the rest in class. | G2.2, P1.5, E3.8 |
| V43 | Characters feel like people through behaviour: a distinctive voice with real phrases, knowing only what they knew then, wanting things from the student, slanting and withholding, remembering, and hedging without contradicting facts. ("Franker in private" was dropped: major characters usually sit in their own rooms, so conversations are already private and the rule would rarely matter.) | Answers the main complaint ("AI responses feel generic") using the feedback's own list. "Wanting something from you" suits a go-between and creates choices about passing things on. | G7, P11–P11.4 |
| V44 | Two tiers of characters: 2–4 major (full depth) and 1–3 minor (light persona, 1–2 discoveries, no stances) per stage. | The feedback asked for more than 4 characters (for example the British in Scene 1). More perspectives cost depth within 15–30 minutes, so minor characters broaden perspectives cheaply. | G4, G8, G8.1 |
| V45 | Characters act only through written behaviour: summons and movement (prioritised, and only through story events, V50), and overheard conversations (low priority, only if easily viable). No free-roaming AI turns. | The earlier attempt at autonomous AI turns was half-built, costly, unpredictable and changed nothing. Written behaviour is plain game logic in which every action changes something. Overheard conversations are nice, but not worth time spent fixing bugs. | G9, P12–P12.2, C8 |
| V46 | Documents: a mix of kinds per stage, real excerpts wherever a source exists (invented only to fill gaps), at least one slanted, incomplete or later-contradicted document per stage, found in rooms, handed over or delivered at events. | The feedback asked for conflicting primary sources with provenance, occasional bias, source diversity, and more verbatim quotes ("reading is a skill"). | G10–G10.2, P13 |
| V47 | Close reading: reading a document doesn't create a note; selecting a passage that overlaps a written key passage does. | Picking up a document was trivial (pillar 7). This trains close reading and inference without exam-style questions, and it's deterministic (an overlap check, no model). Hints can point to the paragraph for the low floor. Similar to Her Story and Return of the Obra Dinn. | G10.3, P13.1–P13.3 |
| V48 | Claims under test (closed; how slants are caught is refined by V69): written character slants, documents that contradict them, no verdict labels during play, hints that prompt cross-checking, confrontation as a trigger, and the reveal confirms what was slanted. | Labels like "contradicted" would do the thinking for the student. Noticing the conflict is the critical thinking; confrontation makes the world push back; the reveal keeps understanding first. Uses only existing mechanics. | G10.4, P13.4, P13.5, E3.5 |
| V49 | Stance visibility: the character's portrait in the conversation changes expression with their stance, plus a short, subtle notice in the story when it changes. No stance names. | Obvious yet subtle: students read the person, not a meter. The notice catches weaker students who miss the face. The current chat already shows a portrait and name. Exact placement is decided later. | G4.2, P6.3–P6.5 |
| V50 | Story events: 2–4 per stage, written, deterministic and editable by the teacher. Triggers: time, condition, stuck, timer runs out. Effects from a fixed list: summons, a character moving, a document delivered, news or an announcement, the defining moment beginning. A "stuck" event creates an opportunity, never an answer. | Gives pacing and makes summons, deliveries, the last hint step and the final call one mechanism. A fixed list of effects keeps it reliable. Limiting the number keeps stages from feeling scripted. The opportunity rule keeps P5.3 (hints never complete progress). | G11, P14–P14.2 |
| V51 | Exploration: keep the walkable 2D map (2–5 rooms per stage). Students can walk automatically to a named room, but not to a person or object. The minimap is always shown and shows the room layout only (no teacher setting). | Walking is thinking time (teacher). Automatic walking to a target turned investigation into following a checklist (pillar 7). Hiding where people and documents are makes exploring meaningful, which the teacher asked for. The existing map infrastructure is reused. | P15–P15.3 |
| V52 | Hints: automatic only. "Stuck" means no progress for a time that adapts to the stage: with a timer, half of (time left ÷ core discoveries left), between 45 seconds and 3 minutes; without a timer, 2 minutes. The clock pauses while reading or waiting for a reply. No message counts. Shown as the role's own thoughts. The teacher sees where the class got stuck, as totals only. | A fixed time ignores time left and what remains, and a dynamic one pushes students to the core before the stage ends. 1 minute without a timer would fire while students are walking or reading. Students would game message counts with random messages. Automatic hints guarantee the low floor (weaker students often don't ask) and leave nothing to spam. The wait is already short, so a manual button isn't needed. | P5.1–P5.1.4, P5.5–P5.7, T2.1 |
| V53 | Prediction: what the real figure will decide because of the student's act; one tap from the defining moment's outcomes (never secret ones, and never already-closed ones, V61); revealed with the reason when the next stage opens (or in the epilogue); never scored. | Trains reasoning about the consequences of one's own act. Reuses written outcomes, so no extra content. Free text couldn't be compared without a model judging it. The rule-based reason ("because the Tunku was alarmed…") is where the learning is. No scoring keeps C4. | P3.2–P3.6, T2.2 |
| V54 | Earlier stages don't branch into separate stages. Their outcomes change the next stage's situation and close specific endings ("effective branching"). The historical ending is reachable from the start, but earlier choices may close it. At least two endings stay reachable at the final defining moment. The reveal shows which choice closed which ending and where the run split from history. | Separate branches would multiply content for every later stage. Closing endings keeps choices meaningful at a manageable cost. Students may genuinely leave the historical path; understanding first is kept because the reveal always leads with what actually happened (E3.1, E3.8) and covers missed core discoveries (E3.7). Showing where the run split is the causal map the feedback asked for. This narrows the earlier reading of Q3 (V37) to "offered while still reachable". | P9.2, P9.8–P9.10, G6.3, E3.6.1 |
| V55 | Endings: 1 historical, 2–3 alternatives and 0–1 secret, as the outcomes of the final defining moment, with no good or bad labels. | "Good" and "bad" endings imply a score (C4), and history isn't winning or losing. Endings differ in what happened, not in how well the student did. | G6.4 |
| V56 | Secret ending: at most one, at the final defining moment; a plausible path history didn't take (cited where possible); unlocked by a hidden go-between act that appears only when 2–3 skilful-play conditions are met (trust, depth, catching a slant, brokering); never hinted at; the reveal mentions that it existed. | The high ceiling (pillar 2) rewards critical thinking actually used. The act's appearance is the signal, with no pop-up. One per adventure keeps generation and checking manageable. Mentioning it in the reveal without explaining how feeds the class discussion ("how did you get that one?"). | G6.1.1, G6.1.2, P9.11, P9.12, E3.6.2 |
| V57 | Sabotage: darker paths chosen through the game's own acts are legitimate alternatives. Made-up claims, helpful or harmful, are challenged and change nothing. Two challenged claims cost a stance with that character. Written deception acts can exist at defining moments where the episode suits it. | The guards against shortcuts must work in both directions. Curiosity about "what if it went wrong" is the contingency lesson, so it should lead somewhere educational rather than nowhere. The trust cost makes lying believable without the game judging truth. It counts challenged claims, not messages, so it doesn't conflict with V52. | P8.2–P8.4, G4.3, P9.13 |
| V58 | Safety: characters never repeat slurs or hateful statements and never break character on instruction; student messages are untrusted input; abusive or provocative messages are flagged and shown to the teacher for that student. | Episodes such as communal politics and riots invite provocation, and this is a Singapore school context. Showing flags to the teacher is safeguarding, not grading. | P16, T5, C9, C10 |
| V59 | Interview push-back: 3 written verdicts per question; for each verdict, written counter-points (discoveries and documents against it) plus a general counter-argument. The rule picks the push-back: the student's own contrary note, then a missed core discovery, then the general counter-argument. The model phrases it. One push-back only, then a close with no judgement. Class totals go to the teacher. | Using the student's own contrary evidence is the fairest and best test of judgement (reconciling conflicting evidence). A missed core discovery teaches at the moment it matters (understanding first). Choosing by rule keeps it reliable; the model only words it. Worked examples: the Merger (historical ending) and the Cuban Missile Crisis (alternative ending). | G6.5, G6.6, E2.4.1, E2.4.2, E2.6, T2.3 |
| V60 | The class summary is rich and easy to navigate: where the class got stuck, how predictions and Interview verdicts split, discoveries found or missed per character, endings reached, selected anonymised Interview answers, and flagged messages, organised into sections (for example, tabs) the teacher can explore freely. Details of how teachers reshape an adventure (T4) move to level 6. | "The more the merrier" for teachers, as long as it's easy to manage. Discoveries missed per character shows which perspectives the class missed. Anonymised answers feed the discussion without exposing students. | T2.4–T2.7 |
| V61 | Four gaps found by writing the example playthrough: angle hints can suggest passing on a note or showing a document; prediction choices exclude closed outcomes as well as secret ones; core discoveries come only from major characters or documents; notes from documents and conversations work the same everywhere. | Without the first, key triggers (such as telling Lee the Tunku's grievance) could only be found by luck. Closed outcomes would be impossible predictions. A core discovery held by a minor character would make understanding depend on someone the student might skip. Document notes were already used as Interview evidence in the playthrough but never stated. | P5.2.1, P3.3, G5.3, P8.5 |
| V62 | Generate in passes, with the skeleton as the shared contract: sources, skeleton (checked straight away), stage details and personas in parallel, endings, reveal, checks with targeted repair. | Today's single call already produces about 23k output tokens against a 32k cap, takes about 200 seconds for planning alone (about 10 minutes end to end in practice), and every repair is a cross-reference or grounding slip. v3 needs roughly 2–4× the content, which in one call would hit output limits, multiply cross-reference errors, degrade late details, and make every repair re-send everything. Input context is not the constraint. | G12–G12.2 |
| V63 | Three generation modes: Quick (review at the end), Standard (also pause after the skeleton; default), Guided (pause after every pass). Generation runs in the background with progress and notifications. Later edits regenerate only what depends on them. | A pause after every pass gives control but makes generation long and hands-on. The skeleton is the one pause worth its cost, because everything else depends on it. Details are cheap to fix later through targeted regeneration. | T4.1, T6, T6.1 |
| V64 | Level 6 is full but focused: the skeleton and the policies (grounding, checks, size limits, editing, reading level) are approved product decisions; the detailed parts of the content model are an engineering draft. | Teachers see and edit the skeleton, so it is product-level. The exact detail structure is an engineering concern. | — |
| V65 | The skeleton holds every structural decision, including the core discoveries, and is checked automatically before the teacher sees it. The teacher can edit anything in it, and the checks re-run after each edit. | Everything in the detail passes depends on the skeleton, so it is where teacher review pays off most. Including core discoveries means teachers explicitly confirm what every student is guaranteed to learn (understanding first). | G12.3, G12.4, T6.2 |
| V66 | Grounding: core discoveries, real documents, real phrases, the historical ending, what actually happened and interpretations must be cited; other discoveries are cited where possible or labelled invented; the role, dialogue, invented documents and alternative endings are labelled invented. Quotes are checked word for word. The teacher's materials win conflicts, which are shown at review. Outside sources come from an allowlist by default, which the teacher can extend or open to any credible cited source. | Core discoveries are what every student is guaranteed to learn, so they can't be invented. Word-for-word checking extends what ingest already does for uploads. An allowlist is safer for schools; the teacher's override covers episodes the allowlist doesn't. | G13–G13.7, T7 |
| V67 | During play, AI handles language and interpretation (free conversation, checks, the interviewer's wording, narration of minor details, moderation); rules own what's true, progress and consequences. No AI resolver, no option minting. | The main complaint was generic characters, not fixed outcomes, so AI spending goes to conversation. Rules make fairness, grounding, teacher review and testing possible. The resolver's jobs split into the rules engine (outcomes), written variants plus model narration (telling), and written acts (no minting, which was about 77% of a typical attempt's cost). Future AI candidates, all inside the rules: bounded persuasion (one stance step on written persuadable points), free-text acts matched to written acts, personalised hint wording, teacher-side summaries of misconceptions. | C11, C12 |
| V68 | Model strength: spend intelligence at generation (frontier for the skeleton, endings, Interview and reveal; frontier or strong mid for details; mid with search for sources). Keep play-time models fast and constrained: strong mid for characters with low reasoning, cheap combined checks off the chat path, mid for the interviewer. Exact models chosen through evaluations. | Generation happens once per adventure and can be checked; play happens every message and needs replies within seconds. Rich personas plus a strong conversational model beat live reasoning: faster, cheaper, more consistent. Cheap models are reliable for narrow judgements against short lists. Removing the resolver, minting and autonomous turns leaves cost headroom for better characters. | G14–G14.3, P17–P17.2 |
| V69 | Slants must be catchable (a document or another character's account) where history provides it, with cited, never invented, contradicting evidence; uncatchable slants are allowed but corrected in the reveal. | Requiring a document for every slant would force invented "proof" against real figures. Another character's account is equally good cross-checking practice. | G10.4, G15 |
| V70 | Reading level, size limits and full consistency checks: Sec or JC chosen at creation; real excerpts verbatim but shorter for Sec, with a glossary; everything else written to level. 5–8 discoveries (2–3 core) and 3–5 documents (1–2 key passages) per stage. Full checks after the detail passes, with targeted repair. | Verbatim excerpts can't be simplified, so length and a glossary do the work. Sizes fit about 15 minutes per stage. The checks make the rules-based design safe to generate. | G15–G17.1 |
| V71 | Teacher editing: a review screen with labels, citations and conflicts; everything editable, directly or by plain-language request that regenerates just that part; real excerpts can be swapped but not rewritten; teacher-written facts labelled as from the teacher; checks re-run after each edit (errors block publishing); preview as a student; edits after publishing create a new version. | "Great ability to reshape" (V33) without breaking grounding or consistency. Plain-language requests suit teachers who don't want to edit rules by hand. Previewing catches what checks can't. Versioning keeps attempts in progress stable, as the current system already does. | T4.2–T4.9 |
| V72 | Review fixes before team review: minor characters may hold slants, and confronting them unlocks a note without a stance change; the session opens with the role briefing; the teacher's own questions come after the reveal, like a take-home or end-of-class question; source labels name the specific document where known, otherwise give the provenance (for example "from the teacher") or "invented"; the PRD is titled "PRD v3". | Triggers belong to major characters only, so a slanted minor character could otherwise never be confronted. The briefing was in the session timings but had no requirement. A named source ("Utusan Melayu, 12 August 1964") is clearer and more useful for source skills than a category, and categories still group them in the reveal. The PRD now covers generation and the teacher console, not just gameplay. | G1.2, G10.4, P13.5, P13.6, P18, E3.4, T1 |

## Team decisions (for the whole team)

| # | Question | Options | Current lean | Impact |

## Level 7 proposals (for team review)

Not yet approved. A1–A14 cover infrastructure: the move off Vercel and Supabase to AWS, with one EC2 instance running a Postgres
container and an app container, S3 for assets, and Supabase kept for sign-in only. They are written to be easy to
grow out of: each one says what changes when one instance is no longer enough. A15–A24 cover generation and gameplay on top of
it.

## Notes for level 7

- With "franker in private" dropped, doors no longer matter for gameplay. Consider simplifying the map (rooms without
  doors to open, close or knock on).
- A portrait expression for every stance (G4.2) means roughly 3–5 portraits per major character instead of 1. Check
  it against the current image generation cost.
- Generation takes ~10 minutes in practice (planning was ~200 seconds in the metrics). First survey: the background
  job is driven by the teacher's browser tab, with one lease per adventure, so steps run one at a time and only
  while a tab polls (`resumable_generation` migration). Passes (G12) need a server-side worker that can run steps
  in parallel. Proposed: a Postgres job queue and worker (A6).
- First reuse/rebuild survey (refined in A15–A24 and the PRD's package table): keep the platform (app shell, auth, ingest, LLM client, map compiler, renderer, asset
  generation, save pattern); rebuild the spec, generation pipeline and worker, play-time orchestration, play session,
  play UI, debrief/reveal, teacher review, and the v3 database tables.
- The move to AWS changes some of the "keep" list: auth stays (Supabase Auth only, A3), but the data layer
  (`supabase-js` queries), asset storage and deploy pipeline are replaced (A5, A8, A12). The asset generation code
  stays behind its `AssetStore` interface.
