THE FOUNDING OF A TRADING POST AT SINGAPORE, 1819
A classroom handout for lower-secondary history

Page 1 — Why the British wanted a new port

After the Napoleonic Wars the Dutch returned to their possessions in the East Indies. Under the Anglo-Dutch Convention of 1814 Britain handed back Java and Malacca, and Dutch influence over the sea lanes between India and China grew again. British merchants trading with China complained that they had no safe harbour of their own at the southern end of the Straits of Malacca.

Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles, the Lieutenant-Governor of the British settlement at Bencoolen on the west coast of Sumatra, argued that the East India Company needed a station commanding the entrance to the China Sea. In late 1818 he obtained permission from the Governor-General in Calcutta, Lord Hastings, to look for a suitable site. His instructions were cautious: he was to establish a post at the southern end of the Straits but to avoid any collision with the Dutch, who were then extending their authority over Riau.

Raffles sailed from Penang with a small squadron. Major William Farquhar, who had been the Resident of Malacca until it was returned to the Dutch, accompanied him and was to command whatever settlement they founded.
Page 2 — Singapore in January 1819

Raffles arrived off the island of Singapore on 28 January 1819 and landed the following day near the mouth of the Singapore River. The island then held a small settlement of perhaps a thousand people, most of them Orang Laut and Malays, with a smaller number of Chinese planters.

Singapore formed part of the Johor Sultanate. The island was administered on the Sultan's behalf by the Temenggong, Abdul Rahman, who had moved to Singapore with his followers a few years earlier. On 30 January 1819 Raffles and the Temenggong signed a preliminary agreement allowing the East India Company to set up a trading post on the island.

The agreement had a weakness. The Temenggong governed Singapore, but he was not the sovereign; only the Sultan of Johor could lawfully grant land to the Company. And the Johor throne itself was disputed.
Page 3 — The disputed throne of Johor

When Sultan Mahmud Shah III of Johor died in 1812 he left two sons. The elder, Tengku Hussein, also known as Tengku Long, was away in Pahang for his wedding when his father died. The younger, Tengku Abdul Rahman, was installed as Sultan in his absence with the support of the powerful Bugis faction at court, and this choice was later recognised by the Dutch.

Raffles took the view that Tengku Hussein, as the elder son, had the stronger claim. The Temenggong sent for Hussein, who was living in obscurity in Riau. Hussein arrived in Singapore in early February 1819, and Raffles recognised him as Sultan Hussein Shah of Johor.

On 6 February 1819 a treaty was signed between the East India Company, represented by Raffles, Sultan Hussein and Temenggong Abdul Rahman. The Company was permitted to establish a factory, or trading post, at Singapore. In return the Sultan was to receive 5,000 Spanish dollars a year and the Temenggong 3,000 Spanish dollars a year. Raffles appointed Farquhar as Resident and Commandant and sailed for Bencoolen on 7 February, having spent little more than a week on the island.
Page 4 — Protest, growth and quarrel

The Dutch protested at once. They regarded Singapore as lying within their sphere and Sultan Abdul Rahman, whom they recognised, as the only lawful ruler of Johor. For several years it was uncertain whether the British government in London would keep the settlement. The dispute was finally settled by the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824, in which the Dutch withdrew their objection to the British presence in Singapore.

Meanwhile the settlement grew faster than anyone had expected. Raffles had declared Singapore a free port, where ships could trade without paying duties, and traders from the region moved their business there. By the time of the first census in January 1824 the population was recorded at 10,683.

Farquhar governed the settlement in Raffles' absence from 1819 to 1823. To raise money he licensed gambling houses and permitted the sale of opium and arrack, and he did not stop the trade in slaves that passed through the port. When Raffles returned in 1822 he disapproved of these measures, redrew the town plan, and in 1823 dismissed Farquhar, replacing him with John Crawfurd.

Questions for discussion
1. Why did Raffles recognise Tengku Hussein rather than deal with Sultan Abdul Rahman?
2. What risks did the Temenggong take by signing the agreement of 30 January 1819?
3. Was the free port policy the main reason Singapore grew so quickly?
